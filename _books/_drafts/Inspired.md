Instead of packing for a big move back up to the San Francisco Bay, in classic Mindy fashion I decided this was the time to pick up a new book! Can someone say productive procrastination?

_Inspired_ has sat unopened on my bookshelf for many years and truthfully I was trying to decide whether to put it in the donation box 🙈 Sitting amongst half-packed boxes, I flipped to page 1 out of curiousity and have been ripping through it since! I'm actually glad I waited until after I had multiple products and teams under my belt to read it. The lessons feel far more visceral and interesting now that they would have 7 years ago.

I'm definitely feeling called out, but also excited because Cagan's ideas align with a lot of the lessons I've learned on the field and resonates with the way I lead.

# Part I: Lessons from Top Tech Companies

### Prototypes

I really like the rule of thumb "a good team should be testing 10-20 product ideas a week."

I love prototyping - in my experience, it's the best and fastest way to spur momentum on a team and get clarity. But it's so hard to get a team to do it, precisely because of the waterfall trap! The one time I was able to set this up, we went from months of ambiguity on what the product was to zero-ing in on on the vision and answering a bunch of questions in the span of 2 weeks. We definitely were in the 10-20 ideas/week velocity during that time. Unfortunately, we were hit with a reorg shortly after. And with the culture that the transfers brought in, the team became more waterfall again.

### Product Delivery

A lot of my teams have struggled immensely with product delivery when I joined, so I've had to spend most of my time here. If you cannot deliver product then all the discovery in the world will do you no good. However, I've observed that a lot of teams get tripped up in the operational transition from discovery to delivery and back as needed -- everyone knows that something isn't working, but there is always huge cultural inertia towards shifting processes towards something new. So my next growth area is to figure out how to set up team norms for both!

### Product Market Fit
I have spent so much of my career in this space that I'm feeling quite confident on it!

### Product Vision
My growth area here is to champion my vision more loudly. I always have a fairly clear vision in my head and some artifacts around it, but rarely have had the time to flesh it out or repeat it as much as one should (see above about how my teams tended to struggle with Product Delivery).

# Part II: The Right People

### Reporting Structure
Here Cagan suggests a flat hierarchy for a team of 1 PM, 1 Designer, and ~10 engineers as a typical team. I'd like to add some suggestions and nuance:

- In my experience adding a dedicated Program Manager helps to keep things more organized and makes the workload much more manageable. Basically, the PgM manages product delivery, whereas the PM focuses on product discovery. Having only one person trying to do both is when things like strong product vision, stakeholder management, or operational optimization gets lost.

- A matrixed hierarchy may work well in large companies where everyone is literate in tech, and going up the reporting chain doesn't actually pull you too far from the context of the product. But for companies where tech is not the main focus (for example, the non-profit I worked at), or in places where not everyone is working towards the same portfolio of products (for example, a governmental digital services consulting agency), in my opinion it is important that everyone on the team reports up to a single person, usually the product manager, in a product-based organizational hierarchy instead of a matrixed one. From my experience, trying to keep a matrixed hierarchy in places where the people managers have little context is actually quite disempowering for all involved:

1. sometimes the managers don't know the product or even tech in general well enough, so they can't give much guidance to their report
2. there's not always another level of tech folks, so the pros of a matrixed oragnization - the ability of influence peers at the next level, is not needed.
3. in these cases, the people manager ends up leaning back on the product manager anyways for guidance on what to tell the report, and what you get is a triangular game of telephone.

Of course, this can go very well or very poorly depending on the personality of the PM. If they are a good PM, giving them people management responsibility is likely to encourage collaboration and trying to bring all their direct reports into the conversation together. If they are not, it gives them even more ammo to be authoritarian.
